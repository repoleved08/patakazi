Most technical job descriptions are written to be scanned, not read. Once you
know what each section is for, you can extract what you need in about two
minutes — and see clearly when a role is not what it claims.

This is the framework we use when we review listings for this board.

## Read the title last

Titles are the least informative part. "AI Engineer", "ML Engineer", and
"Data Scientist" can describe almost anything, and the same title means different
jobs in different companies.

Read the responsibilities first, then check whether the title matches. If the
body is infrastructure work, it is a platform job wearing an AI title, and that
matters for both what you will learn and what it pays.

## The four sections that carry information

**Responsibilities** — the real job. If you cannot find a concrete deliverable
in here, the role is undefined.

**Requirements** — a wish list where everything is required. Watch for a list
long enough to disqualify everyone; that is a sign the hiring manager has not
decided what they need.

**Tech stack** — usually accurate, rarely complete. It tells you the daily
environment, not the actual difficulty of the work.

**Compensation** — the most useful signal on the page, and often the most
deliberately vague. A published range, even a narrow one, tells you they have
thought about the hire. See our
[piece on salary transparency](/blog/salary-transparency) for why we publish
every band.

## Red flags, in rough order of severity

**Security clearance or nationality requirements with no explanation.** Legitimate
in government and defence-adjacent work, and always worth asking about directly
rather than discovering at offer stage.

**"Rockstar", "ninja", "guru".** Low signal at best. It frequently means the
compensation is not competitive and the posting is compensating rhetorically.

**Fifteen required technologies, none of them the hard part.** A team that wants
you to know Kubernetes, Kafka, Spark, Terraform, React, Go, Rust, and Python
usually wants one of those and has not decided which.

**A stack that no longer exists.** Plenty of companies still advertise expertise
in technologies that have been deprecated for a decade. Read the rest of the
posting charitably, but notice it.

**Heavy on buzzwords, silent on the work.** No mention of users, data,
reliability, or trade-offs anywhere in a senior engineering posting is a genuine
warning.

**Compensation listed as "competitive" with no range**, especially for a role
where comparable salaries are public. You can negotiate better with a number
than without one.

**On-call described as "occasional" for a support-heavy role.** Occasional is
relative, and the definition is set after you join.

## Green flags, equally specific

**The band is published and the seniority is defined.** It means someone
modelled the role rather than drafting it from a template.

**Failure and trade-offs are mentioned.** Teams that describe what goes wrong
have operated a system. This is the strongest single signal in a posting.

**The responsibilities name a real user.** Not "stakeholders" in the abstract,
but the kind of person who is affected by the work.

**Interview stages are described, including the format.** A loop of six
unexplained stages is a process the company has not examined.

**On-call is named with a rotation and compensation.** That is what honesty
about on-call looks like.

## What to ask in the first call

You are entitled to ask, and these are not unreasonable:

1. What does success in this role look like at six months?
2. What is the current on-call load, and how is it compensated?
3. How does the team decide what to build next?
4. What went wrong with the previous person in this role, if there was one?
5. What is the salary range for this level?

Question two gets a surprising amount of information. Question four gets you a
lot of honesty from teams that respect the question.

## A worked example

**Weak posting:** "We are looking for a passionate AI Engineer to join our
dynamic team. You will work on cutting-edge LLM solutions with our
rockstar team. Requirements: 5+ years Python, ML, DL, NLP, computer vision,
Kubernetes, AWS, GCP, Azure, Spark, Kafka. Apply with an exciting opportunity to
grow."

**What you learn from it:** almost nothing about the work, and a strong signal
about the recruiter. Whether to apply depends entirely on whether you want to
work there, not whether you are suitable.

**Stronger posting for the same role:** the same title, with responsibilities
that name a product, a team size, a real model-serving problem, a published
band, a defined interview loop, and an honest note about on-call.

The difference is not seniority. It is whether anyone involved in the work
wrote it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many jobs should I apply to?**
Enough that you get conversations, few enough that you can write something real
for each. Generic applications perform badly in technical hiring.

**Should I apply if I meet 70% of the requirements?**
Usually yes, provided you meet the core of it. The list is inflated by
assumption, and the interesting part of a good posting is which few skills are
genuinely central.

**Is a posting that lists salaries more likely to be a good employer?**
Not necessarily, but it does mean someone has thought about the role, and it
makes it far easier for you to self-select.

**How do I spot a rehash of an old posting?**
Specific dates that have passed, technology that has moved on, and a "we are
growing our team" line that has survived several reissues.
