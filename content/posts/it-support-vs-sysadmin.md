These are the two most common entry points into IT, they are routinely offered at
the same entry level, and they lead to genuinely different careers. Getting the
distinction right early saves a couple of years.

## The honest difference

**IT support** is a service role. Users have a problem, you resolve it. The work
is triage, communication, and a broad shallow knowledge of everything from a
laptop to a printer to a VPN.

**Systems administration** is an infrastructure role. Systems must stay up. The
work is Linux, networking, storage, identity, and the automation that keeps
repeating work from consuming your week.

Both can pay well. The systems route has a higher ceiling and a slower start.

## Side by side

| | IT support | Systems administrator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary interface | People | Systems |
| Core skills | Windows/macOS, networking basics, ticketing, communication | Linux, networking, storage, scripting, virtualisation |
| Success looks like | Users unblocked, tickets closed | Uptime, fewer recurring incidents, automation |
| Pace | Interrupt-driven, high volume | Project and maintenance driven |
| Certifications | CompTIA A+, Network+, Microsoft fundamentals | RHCSA/RHCE, Linux+, VMware, cloud associate |
| Typical ceiling | Service desk lead, IT manager | Senior sysadmin, SRE, platform engineer |
| Strongest signal | Writing clearly under pressure | Automating your own repetitive work |

## What IT support is really like

The perception is that it is a dead end. It can be, if you never move. What
makes it a good launchpad is the breadth: in a year you will have touched more
systems than a specialist does in five.

**What actually matters:** troubleshooting method rather than memorised fixes,
writing a ticket someone else can act on, and the social skill of asking a
frustrated person questions without making it worse. The technical ceiling is
real — most support work is not intellectually demanding.

**How to avoid the ceiling:** automate something every month, learn scripting
properly, and ask to touch infrastructure rather than only endpoints.

## What sysadmin work is really like

Fewer people, more responsibility. You are the person who gets paged.

**What actually matters:** Linux in depth, networking you can debug from a
packet capture, scripting in Python or Bash, and understanding virtualisation and
identity. Storage is the area people underestimate — filesystem behaviour,
quotas, snapshots, and what actually happens during a restore.

**The culture shock:** exposure. A support role can be quiet at 3pm. A sysadmin
role is a pager. Being on call is a real lifestyle cost and worth pricing in.

## Where the money sits

Both roles are highly variable, and location matters more than title.

- Support tends to be flatter: a band, few rungs, and the ceiling is usually
  management.
- Sysadmin has a longer ladder: senior, lead, architect, and then the cloud or
  platform track.
- Specialised regulated environments — telecoms, banking, healthcare — pay a
  premium precisely because the on-call is harder.
- Contract and freelance rates are higher than permanent equivalents, with less
  security.

## How to choose

**Choose support if** you like people, are good at explaining things, and want
to learn a lot of systems quickly. Choose a role where the employer will let you
escalate into infrastructure.

**Choose sysadmin if** you enjoy systems, would rather not spend your day
negotiating, and can tolerate being on call.

**Choose neither yet if** you are unsure: a helpdesk or support role where you
can script, plus a home lab, plus a cloud certification, is a good twelve-month
plan that keeps every door open.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is IT support a dead end?**
Only if you stay in it without automating. It is an excellent way to see many
systems quickly, and it is how a lot of good sysadmins started.

**Do I need a degree?**
Rarely in either role. A home lab, a documented project, and a relevant
certification carry more weight.

**Is on-call worth it for the pay?**
Sometimes. The premium is real, but so is the interruption. Price both.

**How do I get Linux experience without a job?**
Build something: a small homelab with containers, a reverse proxy, backups you
have actually restored, and monitoring you have actually used at 2am. Document
it. That is a credible portfolio.
